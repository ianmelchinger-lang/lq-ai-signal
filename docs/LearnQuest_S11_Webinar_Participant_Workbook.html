Build a cost-of-inaction figure you could defend in front of their CFO

Pick one live opportunity that has gone quiet — not your best deal. Fill the four boxes below. Every number needs a source before this workbook will calculate anything, which is the entire point of the exercise.

The rule
Every input is Stated, Published, Benchmarked, or Assumed-and-agreed. There is no fifth category.
Stated
The customer said it, in a meeting you can name, on a date you can name.
Published
It's in a document anyone can look up — annual report, job posting, their own website.
Benchmarked
An external figure you can produce on request, with the source named at the time you use it.
Assumed & agreed
A judgement the customer has explicitly accepted — and that you label as an assumption every time you show it.
If you can't fill a box, leave it blank and write down the question you'd have to ask to fill it. A blank box with a question against it is a next step in the deal. A guessed box is a liability.
The AI step

You hand it your notes. It builds the four-part model, does the arithmetic, and names every slot it had to fill without a source.

The arithmetic is mechanical and the tool does not make slips in it. The genuinely valuable behaviour is that it will leave a slot blank and say why, where a rep under time pressure fills it with something plausible and forgets by the second draft that they did. The tool has no such incentive.

IN
Your discovery notes for one live opportunity, pasted in unedited.

The model

Four boxes

Work top to bottom. The figure at the bottom updates as you go, but only once every input in the chain carries a source.

1
The leak
What does the problem cost them per week, today?
Volume × effort × loaded cost. Keep the period weekly — people can tell you what a normal week looks like, and almost nobody can tell you an accurate annual figure off the top of their head. The tool does this arithmetic and quotes the sentence behind each input. It will reach for a loaded hourly rate, because that is almost never in anyone's notes — go and replace it with a benchmark you can produce.

The recoverable share
How much of that leak would actually go away?
Never all of it. This is almost always an assumption, which makes it the number to put to the customer out loud before it appears anywhere in writing. If you haven't agreed it with them yet, that's your next step — say so in the note. The tool will quote whatever sentence it found here. Read the sentence: it is often you proposing a percentage and the customer not objecting. That is Assumed and agreed, not Stated.

3
The delay
How long until a decision realistically lands?
Ask them — do not guess. Their own procurement cycle is a stated input, and it is almost always longer than your champion first tells you. If you're guessing this, the whole figure is a guess. The tool uses whatever window is in your notes — including your own forecast date, if that is what you pasted. Only you know whether the window is theirs or yours.
4
The dated event
Is there a date inside that window that changes the cost?
A renewal, an escalator, an audit, a contract that auto-renews, a migration window that closes. This box does not change the arithmetic — it is what turns a number into a reason to move. A number argues; a date decides. Ask for it explicitly and the tool will find the date sitting unnoticed in your notes, which is the cheapest win in the session. It cannot find one you never wrote down.
Leave this blank if there genuinely isn't one. An invented deadline is the fastest way to lose the room.
